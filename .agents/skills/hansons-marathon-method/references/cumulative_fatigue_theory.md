# Cumulative Fatigue Theory (Hansons Marathon Method)

This reference outlines the physiological and biomechanical rationale behind cumulative fatigue and why Hansons caps the long run at 16 miles.

---

## 1. The Physiological Flaw of the 20+ Mile Long Run

In traditional marathon plans, runners peak with 20 to 22-mile long runs. However, for a 3:45 to 4:30+ marathoner, running 20 miles in training takes 3.0 to 4.0+ hours.

Physiological research demonstrates:
1. **Diminishing Returns After 2.5–3 Hours**: Beyond 2.5 to 3 hours of continuous running, cellular aerobic adaptation plateaus, while soft-tissue damage (microtears, inflammation, joint stress) increases exponentially.
2. **Post-Long Run Hangover**: A single exhausting 20-miler often requires 4 to 6 days of sluggish recovery, forcing subsequent training days to become low quality.
3. **Biomechanical Breakdown**: As muscle glycogen depletes near mile 17–18 of an isolated long run, running form deteriorates, reinforcing inefficient movement patterns and drastically increasing acute injury risk (IT band friction, plantar fasciitis, stress reactions).

---

## 2. The Solution: Cumulative Fatigue

Rather than seeking adaptation in a single heroic run, Hansons spreads the training load across 6 days of consistent running:
- The runner completes Tuesday Speed/Strength intervals.
- The runner completes Thursday Marathon Pace Tempo (up to 10 miles).
- Easy runs fill the gaps (Monday, Wednesday, Friday, Saturday).
- When the runner lines up for Sunday's 16-mile long run, their glycogen stores are already partially depleted, and their legs are moderately fatigued.
- **The Result**: The 16-mile run replicates the physiological and psychological environment of **miles 10 through 26** of race day, without the orthopedic damage of running 20–22 miles in one session.

---

## 3. Weekly Volume Distribution Rule

Hansons dictates that the long run must remain a proportional component of the weekly whole:
$$\text{Long Run Distance} \le 25\% - 33\% \text{ of Weekly Total}$$

If a runner only runs 35 miles a week, their long run should be no more than 10–12 miles. To safely run 16 miles, total weekly mileage must reach 50–65 miles per week.
